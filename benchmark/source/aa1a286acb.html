D-01 / League story

After work, the lights come on.

In Humboldt the week still ends on a diamond. Hardware clerks, line cooks, bank tellers, and shift men from the rail side of town pull on wool and polyester that carry the name of the store that paid for it. Harris Hardware has been on the jersey since 1947. Parker Drug still smells like the soda fountain even if the fountain is gone. The league is not a brand. It is a civic habit.

The four fields sit inside neighborhoods, not a complex. Riverside takes the river wind. East Park backs up to the school. Foundry was graded from the old mill lot. Fairgrounds still shares a fence with the cattle barn. Kids chase foul balls into the lot. Grandparents keep score in the same notebooks their parents used. Nobody needs a video board to know who is winning.

Grounds crews are volunteers. They drag the infield after supper, chalk the boxes in the last of the daylight, and argue quietly about whether the warning track needs more brick dust. When a storm sits over Gibson County, Field 03 goes to rain hold and the crew waits it out with coffee from Main Street Market. Maintenance on Field 04 this week is a sprinkler head and a soft spot behind third, not a crisis.

Friday night is the institution. First pitch at Riverside is 6:10, which is late enough for the stores to close and early enough that the third shift can still catch the late innings. Warmups sound like aluminum and chatter. The All-Star break in July still shuts half of Main Street for a weekend; the banquet is in the VFW hall and the game is at Fairgrounds, lights humming, moths in the beams. Competition is real. So is the ride home.

This board is the digital child of the mechanical one that hung at Riverside from 1981 until the city wired the parks office. Same hinge. Same patience. Same refusal to make a spectacle of a run.
